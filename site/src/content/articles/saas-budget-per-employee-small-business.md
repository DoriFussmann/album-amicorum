## What Should You Actually Be Spending on SaaS?

If you run a small business and you've never added up every software subscription on your company card, the total will likely surprise you. SaaS budget per employee is one of the most undertracked costs in small business finance — and one of the easiest places to quietly bleed cash.

This guide gives you a practical framework to benchmark your current spend, decide what's worth keeping, and build a software budget that scales with your team rather than ahead of it.

## The Benchmark: What Do Small Businesses Typically Spend?

Industry data consistently puts average SaaS spend somewhere between $3,000 and $6,000 per employee per year across small and mid-sized businesses. That wide range exists for a reason: the right number depends heavily on your industry, your team's roles, and how tech-dependent your business model is.

A five-person professional services firm might run lean at $2,500 per employee. A ten-person e-commerce operation with a marketing stack, inventory system, and customer support platform could easily hit $5,000 or more.

Neither number is inherently right or wrong. What matters is whether each tool earns its place.

**A rough starting benchmark by business type:**

- Service businesses (consulting, agencies, legal): $2,000–$4,000 per employee
- E-commerce or product businesses: $3,500–$6,000 per employee
- SaaS or tech-enabled businesses: $5,000–$8,000 per employee

Use these as a reality check, not a target to optimize toward.

## Why SaaS Costs Creep Up Without You Noticing

Subscription costs grow differently from most business expenses. You don't approve them every month — you approve them once, and then they recur. Over time, a few patterns tend to drive costs up:

**Tool duplication.** Two team members solve the same problem with different tools and neither realizes it. You end up paying for Slack and Teams, or Notion and Asana, simultaneously.

**Seat bloat.** You hire, add seats, grow — but rarely shrink the subscription when someone leaves or a team restructures.

**The "just in case" subscription.** A tool gets added for a project, the project ends, and the subscription quietly continues for months.

**Free trials that convert.** Someone on your team starts a trial, forgets to cancel, and the card on file gets charged when the trial ends.

None of these are irresponsible decisions. They're the natural result of a fast-moving small business where no one person owns the software stack.

## How to Audit Your SaaS Stack in Under Two Hours

Before you set a forward-looking budget, get a clear view of where you stand today. Here's a practical process:

1. **Pull your bank and credit card statements** for the last three months. Filter for recurring charges. Note the tool name, monthly or annual cost, and who requested it.
2. **Map each subscription to a business function** — communication, sales, marketing, operations, finance, HR, and so on.
3. **Estimate actual usage.** If the tool has a login history or usage dashboard, check it. If no one can remember the last time they logged in, that's your answer.
4. **Assign an owner.** Every subscription should have a named person responsible for it. If it doesn't have an owner, it doesn't have a clear business case.
5. **Make a keep/cancel/review list.** Keep what's clearly earning its cost, cancel what's dormant, and flag anything that needs a deeper look.

Most small businesses that do this exercise for the first time find they can cut 15–25% of their SaaS spend immediately — often within a single billing cycle.

## Building a SaaS Budget by Role, Not by Headcount

The most common mistake founders make when setting a SaaS budget is dividing a round number evenly across all employees. Software needs vary dramatically by role.

A salesperson needs a CRM, a prospecting tool, an e-signature tool, and likely a scheduling app. A designer needs entirely different software. Applying one flat number to both creates the wrong incentive — either the salesperson is under-resourced or you're over-allocating budget to roles that only need a handful of tools.

Instead, build a **role-based software bundle** for each function in your business:

- Define the core tools each role genuinely needs
- Price out the annual cost of those tools per seat
- Use that as the software budget for that function

When you hire, you're adding a known software cost alongside salary, benefits, and equipment. That's a much cleaner way to model the true cost of headcount growth.

## The Prioritization Framework: Must-Have vs. Nice-to-Have

When resources are limited, not every tool competes equally for your budget. A simple way to prioritize is to ask two questions about each subscription:

**Would losing this tool require a significant manual workaround?** If yes, it's load-bearing — keep it. If not, it's optional.

**Does this tool directly support revenue or reduce a meaningful operational cost?** If the answer is no to both, the burden of proof for keeping it is high.

Tools that typically survive this test for small businesses include: your primary communication platform, project management software, your financial operations tool (invoicing, payroll, or accounting), and the CRM or customer support system that sits at the center of your business.

Tools that often don't survive the test: the third analytics platform, the duplicate video conferencing tool, the social media scheduler nobody uses, and the AI writing tool someone added during a product launch and never revisited.

## Setting Your Annual SaaS Budget: A Simple Formula

Once you've audited your current spend and mapped tools to roles, you can set a forward-looking budget using this approach:

**Annual SaaS budget = (current validated spend) + (planned headcount adds × role-based software cost) + (10–15% buffer for new tools)**

The buffer matters. You will adopt new tools during the year. Building in room for that — intentionally — is better than constantly approving ad-hoc subscriptions that never get reviewed.

Revisit this budget quarterly. Software needs evolve, headcount changes, and the SaaS market moves fast. A number set in January may not reflect your business in October.

## The Bottom Line

Setting a SaaS budget per employee isn't about spending as little as possible — it's about spending deliberately. The businesses that get the most from their software stack aren't the ones with the most tools. They're the ones that know exactly what they're paying for, why it's there, and who owns it.

Start with an audit, build by role, and review quarterly. That discipline alone puts most small businesses well ahead of where they started.
